# Day Time: An Evidence-Graded Work Protocol for ADHD, Early Perimenopause, and a Levonorgestrel IUD (32-Day Cycle)

Yes, you can sensibly plan work around your cycle. But the evidence supports using cycle phase to manage load and protect mood, more than it supports predicting peak brain performance on given days. The most solid signal for a woman with ADHD is that symptoms and emotional dysregulation get worse when estradiol falls. That happens in the days around bleeding and just after ovulation. Your hormonal IUD hides the bleeding cue, and perimenopause makes the timing less predictable. So your schedule has to be anchored to ovulation markers (LH strips, temperature) and to your own daily ratings, not to the calendar.

## TL;DR

- **What is well established vs. not:** Estradiol modulates prefrontal dopamine. Falling estradiol around menstruation predicts worse mood and, in small studies, worse ADHD symptoms. Women with ADHD report premenstrual dysphoric disorder (PMDD) symptoms at much higher rates (45.5% in one clinic sample of 209). Evidence that cognitive *performance* reliably peaks by cycle phase is weak: a 2025 meta-analysis of 102 articles found "no systematic robust evidence" of cycle shifts in cognitive performance. Almost all of this research is in women under 40, most without ADHD, and none in women your age who use an LNG-IUD.
- **What your situation adds:** At 43–44, likely early perimenopause makes estradiol *more erratic* (often higher peaks, not just lower) and can weaken or skip ovulation. A 32-day cycle usually means ovulation around day 18, with the luteal phase staying about 12–14 days. Your LNG-IUD adds a small, steady dose of levonorgestrel and usually leaves ovulation intact (most Kyleena users ovulate; the FDA Mirena label reports about 45% of cycles ovulatory in a 1-year study and 75% after 4 years). It also makes bleeding an unreliable day-1 marker.
- **What to do:** Use three zones:
  - **Green (roughly days 6–18):** deep and creative work, high-stakes communication.
  - **Amber (days 19–29):** execution, structure, review.
  - **Red / buffer (days 30–32 and 1–3):** low demand, admin, planning.

  Re-anchor every cycle to a positive LH test or a confirmed temperature rise. Layer the non-medication basics on top (sleep protection, exercise before hard work, external structure). Track focus, mood and energy daily for 2–3 cycles, then let your own data overwrite this template.

## Part 1 — Deep Dive

### 1. ADHD across the female life stages, with emphasis on the early-to-mid 40s

**The mechanism (well supported in animals and healthy women; inferred for ADHD).** Estradiol boosts dopamine signaling in the prefrontal cortex, which is the system that supports working memory, inhibition, and sustained attention. Jacobs and D'Esposito (2011) scanned 24 healthy women at low- and high-estradiol points in the cycle. They found that estradiol changed prefrontal activity and working-memory performance *depending on baseline dopamine tone* (indexed by COMT genotype):\[12\]
- Women with presumed low prefrontal dopamine (Val/Val) improved at high estradiol.
- Women with presumed high prefrontal dopamine (Met/Met) got worse.\[13\]

A 2023 replication with dense sampling across a full cycle confirmed that within-person estradiol changes interact with COMT to predict working memory.\[14\] This "inverted-U" matters for you. More estrogen is not always better.\[15\] A brain with lower baseline dopamine, which is the leading model of ADHD, is the kind that should benefit from higher estradiol and suffer most from its withdrawal. That is an inference, not a tested ADHD finding.

**Life stages (mostly review-level and self-report evidence).** The 2025 Eunethydis consensus review on female ADHD (Kooij, de Jong, Borg Skoglund, and about 40 co-authors) concludes that "hormonal transitions exacerbate ADHD symptoms and mood disturbances, yet pharmacological research and tailored treatments are lacking."\[16\]
- **Puberty:** the "double whammy" theory (Eng, Eisenlohr-Moul, Martel et al., 2024, *Hormones and Behavior*) proposes that girls with ADHD face both organizational and activational hormone effects.\[17\]
- **Postpartum:** Dorani et al. (2021) found postpartum-depression symptoms after first childbirth in 40.6% of 209 women with ADHD.\[18\]
- **Perimenopause:** the review notes postmenopausal subjective cognitive decline involving attention, verbal and working memory.\[19\] It also notes that sleep loss, menopause, and ADHD each worsen executive function, so their effects plausibly stack.\[20\]

**Perimenopause specifically: the honest picture is mixed.**
- **Supporting worsening:** An Icelandic population-based cohort (SAGA; Jakobsdóttir Smári et al., *European Psychiatry*, 2025) compared 535 women with ADHD to 4,857 without. Women with ADHD had higher perimenopausal symptom scores (18.0 vs 13.0) and more severe symptoms (54.2% vs 30.1%; prevalence ratio 1.80). The prevalence of severe symptoms peaked at ages 35–39 in women with ADHD versus 45–49 in women without, which the authors read as an earlier perimenopause onset in ADHD. That age window is directly relevant to you.
- **Not supporting worsening:** A 2025 *Journal of Attention Disorders* study of 656 women aged 45–60 found *no* significant effect of an ADHD diagnosis on menopausal symptoms after correction for multiple comparisons. It did find ADHD symptoms correlated with menopausal complaints across everyone. The authors raise the possibility that women with ADHD attribute symptoms differently.\[6\]\[21\]
- **Surveys:** Jeanette Wasserstein and colleagues' survey of women aged 46+ (presented at the International Neuropsychological Society) found 61% said ADHD had its greatest life impact between 40 and 59. The largest group (43%) was first diagnosed at 41–50, and "poor memory/brain fog" and "feeling overwhelmed" rose most.\[22\] This is a self-selected survey, not a controlled study. Wasserstein herself has written that there are essentially no studies that specifically examine menopause in females with ADHD.\[23\]
- **Clinical framing:** a 2026 review of pharmacological management (*Drugs & Aging*) states plainly that "a systematic review found no empirical studies that investigated ADHD during menopause." It describes the typical clinical presentation as worsening inattention, emotional dysregulation, anxiety, and sleep disruption.\[24\]

**Bottom line for 43–44:** expect your executive function to feel *less stable* rather than simply lower. Expect sleep to become the main amplifier. Treat claims that "perimenopause makes ADHD X% worse" as unproven.

Lotta Borg Skoglund's main contributions here are:
- **Swedish register research:** women are diagnosed 3.9 years later than men (mean age 23.5 vs 19.6 years at first ADHD record; Skoglund et al., 2024, *J Child Psychol Psychiatry*). Her group also produced the Lundin 2023 contraception–depression study (Section 4).\[4\]\[25\]
- **A 2026 qualitative study** ("Controlled by Female Hormones"), which found the premenstrual phase is the most challenging time for women with ADHD.\[4\]
- **A 2026 book with gynecologist Helena Kopp Kallner,** *Female Hormones and ADHD*.\[26\] The book is a synthesis, not primary evidence.

### 2. ADHD across the menstrual cycle

**The studies, in order of relevance to you:**

1. **Roberts, Eisenlohr-Moul & Martel (2018), *Psychoneuroendocrinology*.**
   - **Design:** 32 regularly cycling young women gave daily saliva hormones and evening ADHD symptom ratings for 35 days.\[1\]
   - **Findings:** Falling estradiol *together with* high progesterone or testosterone predicted more ADHD symptoms the *next day*, especially in women high in trait impulsivity. Symptoms rose in the **early follicular** and **early luteal (post-ovulatory)** phases.\[1\]
   - **Limit:** women with ADHD were not selected, and stimulant use was an exclusion criterion.\[27\]\[28\]
2. **Zaritsky, Reed & Evans (*J Atten Disord*, online Dec 2025).**
   - **Design:** 30 women *with diagnosed ADHD on amphetamine salts* completed 35 daily surveys.\[2\]
   - **Findings:** ADHD symptoms were most severe **during menstruation** and mildest **mid-follicular**. The size of that swing tracked changes in negative mood. Amphetamine doses stayed steady across the cycle.\[2\]\[29\]
   - **Limits:** small, self-report only, medicated, no hormonal contraception users.\[28\]\[30\]
3. **A 2026 narrative review of 29 studies (PMC).** Four studies in women with ADHD and six in women with PMS/PMDD "consistently observed impairments in attention, executive function, and impulsivity during the mid-luteal and pre-menstrual phases."\[31\]
4. **Osianlis et al. (2025) systematic review, 11 studies (*J Atten Disord*).** Inattention may be related to falling estrogen, moderated by progesterone. Hyperactivity/impulsivity may be driven by falling estrogen without a progesterone effect.\[32\]
5. **de Jong et al. (2023), *Frontiers in Psychiatry*.** A community case series of 9 women whose premenstrual worsening improved when their stimulant dose was raised in the premenstrual week.\[33\] This is useful as a signal, but it is a case series with no control group.

**PMDD overlap (a risk flag you should take seriously):**
- Dorani et al. (2021): 45.5% of 209 adult ADHD outpatients screened positive for PMDD symptoms, against a pooled general-population prevalence of 3.2% for confirmed PMDD and 7.7% for provisional diagnosis (Reilly et al., 2024, *J Affect Disord*; 44 studies, 50,659 participants).
- Broughton et al. (2025, *British Journal of Psychiatry*), 715 participants: provisional PMDD in 41.1% of those meeting symptom-based ADHD criteria and 31.4% of those with a self-reported diagnosis, vs 9.8% of controls.\[3\]

Both used screening and self-report rather than the prospective 2-cycle daily ratings that PMDD diagnosis requires.\[34\] That is exactly what your app could produce.

**Mechanisms by hormone:**
- **Estradiol withdrawal is the best-supported trigger.** Eisenlohr-Moul's crossover RCT gave estradiol plus progesterone perimenstrually. That prevented the perimenstrual worsening of suicidal ideation and depressed mood. In a companion analysis, estradiol prevented perimenstrual drops in **working memory and verbal fluency**.\[35\]\[36\] This is causal evidence, though in a psychiatric sample without ADHD. Estradiol falls twice per cycle: briefly after ovulation, and steeply before menses.
- **Progesterone and allopregnanolone:** progesterone's metabolite allopregnanolone acts on GABA-A receptors.\[37\] In susceptible people, it is the *change* in levels rather than the absolute level that provokes symptoms.\[35\]\[38\] The emerging framework, which is plausible but not settled, is:
  - mid-luteal hormone surge → irritability and hyperarousal
  - perimenstrual withdrawal → low mood and cognitive dysfunction\[39\]
- **Heart rate variability (HRV), an emotion-regulation biomarker:** a meta-analysis by Schmalenberger and Eisenlohr-Moul showed vagally mediated HRV falls in the luteal phase, with medium-to-large effects, driven mainly by progesterone. Individual differences are large.\[40\]\[41\]

**Where the evidence disagrees, and what I conclude:** Roberts found an *early luteal* bump. Zaritsky found *menstruation* worst. The narrative review emphasizes *mid-luteal and premenstrual*. These are not contradictory: they all point to **phases of falling estradiol**. Rather than betting on one "worst week," the protocol treats the estradiol-drop windows as caution zones:
- the post-ovulation dip (days 19–21 in your model)
- the premenstrual-to-early-menses withdrawal (days 30–32 and 1–3)

### 3. Perimenopause at 43–44 and your 32-day cycle

**Staging.** Under STRAW+10, the early menopausal transition begins with a *persistent* difference of 7 days or more between consecutive cycles (recurring within 10 cycles). The late transition begins with 60 or more days without a period.\[42\]\[43\] With an LNG-IUD, you often can't see your cycle length, and STRAW authors note the criteria are hard to apply to hormonal-contraception users.\[44\] So "likely early perimenopause" at your age is reasonable but can't be confirmed by bleeding alone.

**What happens hormonally (well established from SWAN, the Melbourne cohort, and Santoro's and Hale's work):**
- **FSH rises** as inhibin B falls, accelerating follicle growth and **shortening the follicular phase**.\[45\]\[46\]
- **Estradiol is preserved or even higher, but erratic.** Santoro's group documented erratic estrogen secretion. Hale et al. (2009, *Menopause*) found that 11 of 29 ovulatory cycles in the early and late transition (37%) showed "luteal out-of-phase" (LOOP) events: a second estradiol surge in the mid-to-late luteal phase that peaked during the next menses. These were accompanied by lower luteal progesterone and higher FSH.\[45\]\[47\]
- **Progesterone declines and luteal phases become inadequate.**\[48\] Anovulatory cycles become more common.

**What this means for a 32-day cycle.** In the Natural Cycles dataset (Bull et al., 2019, *npj Digital Medicine*; 612,613 cycles):
- Differences in cycle length came predominantly from the follicular phase.\[49\]\[50\]
- Mean follicular phase was 16.9 days; mean luteal phase was 12.4 days. The luteal phase "varied very little between age cohorts."\[50\]\[51\]
- Cycles shortened by about 3.2 days from age 25 to 45.\[49\]\[50\]

Your 32-day cycle therefore most likely reflects a long follicular phase (about 18 days), with ovulation around day 18–19. Two honest wrinkles:
1. The real-world average luteal phase is about 12–13 days, not 14.\[49\]\[52\] Your premenstrual drop could arrive around day 30 rather than day 32.
2. Perimenopause usually *shortens* cycles first. A 32-day cycle at 43 may be your lifelong baseline, or it may be one of the variable cycles. Your app should log every cycle length.

### 4. The levonorgestrel IUD (Mirena, Kyleena, Liletta, Skyla)

**Terminology.** These devices release **levonorgestrel (LNG)**, a synthetic progestin (a testosterone-derived 19-nortestosterone compound). They do not release progesterone. This matters: LNG does not convert to allopregnanolone the way progesterone does, and it binds SHBG.

**Dose and systemic exposure (FDA and regulatory labels):**

| Device | LNG content | Initial release | Later release | Serum LNG |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mirena | 52 mg | ~20 mcg/day | ~half by year 5; ~9 mcg/day by year 6 | Highest of the group\[53\] |
| Liletta | 52 mg | ~19.5 mcg/day | avg ~14.3 mcg/day over 6 yrs | Similar to Mirena\[53\] |
| Kyleena | 19.5 mg | ~17.5 mcg/day | avg ~9 mcg/day over 5 yrs\[53\] | 162 pg/mL at day 7 → ~91 at 3 yrs → ~83 at 5 yrs\[54\] |
| Skyla/Jaydess | 13.5 mg | ~14 mcg/day | avg ~6 mcg/day over 3 yrs\[53\] | Lowest |

The Kyleena label notes that more than 90% of the released LNG is systemically available, but tissue gradients keep serum levels very low (endometrium-to-serum gradient above 1000-fold).\[8\] SHBG falls about 30% in the first 3 months after insertion.\[55\]\[56\] **Net:** a low, near-steady progestin background that tapers slowly over years, not a cyclic signal.

**Ovulation (key fact for your protocol):**
- **Mirena:** per the FDA label, approximately **45% of cycles were ovulatory in a 1-year study** and **75% after 4 years** in another study.
- **Kyleena:** evidence of ovulation in **23 of 26 women in year 1, 19 of 20 in year 2, and all 16 in year 3**.\[57\]
- **Comparison:** a pooled comparison reported anovulation in 22% of Mirena users vs 8.6% (19.5 mg) and 5.9% (Jaydess).\[58\]

The Kyleena label states there is "no clear difference in follicle development, ovulation, or estradiol and progesterone production in women with different bleeding patterns." Bleeding changes reflect direct action on the endometrium, "not … suppression of the ovulatory cycle."\[55\]\[59\] In other words, your ovaries are probably still cycling underneath. Older small studies suggest LNG-IUD ovulatory cycles may have somewhat lower progesterone peaks; treat that as preliminary.\[60\]

**Bleeding:** irregular bleeding and spotting are common for 3–6 months after insertion. Per the FDA Mirena label, amenorrhea develops in approximately 20% of users by one year. By the end of year 8, 34% have amenorrhea and 26% have infrequent bleeding. **Conclusion:** bleeding cannot be trusted as cycle day 1.

**Mood, depression, and ADHD:**
- Skovlund et al. (2016, *JAMA Psychiatry*), 1,061,997 Danish women aged 15–34 (mean follow-up 6.4 years): LNG-IUD users had a rate ratio of **1.4 (95% CI 1.31–1.42)** for first antidepressant use vs non-users. Risk was highest in adolescents. The study is observational, the absolute risk is small, and it covered younger women than you.
- Lundin, Kopp Kallner, Borg Skoglund et al. (2023, *JAACAP*), 29,767 Swedish girls and women with ADHD aged 15–24:
  - ADHD alone carried an aHR of **3.69** for depression regardless of contraception.\[61\]
  - The *excess* risk with hormonal contraception was concentrated in **oral** contraceptives.
  - Non-oral methods, including hormonal IUDs, did not show a differential effect in ADHD (per the Duke Center summary).\[62\]
- No study has examined cognition or ADHD symptom fluctuation in LNG-IUD users.

**My opinion:** at 43, with an IUD that probably isn't driving your mood, the IUD is a net asset. It also has a forward-looking advantage. Mirena (52 mg) is licensed in some countries (e.g., the UK) for endometrial protection during estrogen therapy.\[58\] If you later add estradiol for perimenopausal symptoms, the progestin half of that regimen may already be in place. Discuss with your clinician.

**Tracking phase with an LNG-IUD (practical):**
- **LH strips (primary anchor):** test daily from about day 13 of your estimated cycle. The first positive = LH surge; ovulation typically follows within about 24–36 hours.\[37\] Caveat: in perimenopause, baseline LH/FSH can rise and some cycles produce multiple or no surges. A positive without a following temperature rise should not be trusted alone.
- **Basal body temperature or a wearable temperature tracker (confirmation):** a sustained rise of about 0.2–0.3 °C for 3 or more days confirms ovulation has *already happened*. The Natural Cycles dataset used exactly this BBT + LH combination to date ovulation.\[63\] Wearables (ring or wrist) are more convenient than oral thermometers but should be treated as estimates.
- **Cervical mucus:** works with LNG-IUDs only partially, because LNG thickens cervical mucus locally. Low value for you.
- **Symptom tracking:** breast tenderness, libido shifts, and mid-cycle pain support the other markers but are not anchors.

### 5. Non-medication strategies (and medication context)

**Graded evidence for unmedicated adults with ADHD:**
- **Exercise (moderate evidence; best "acute" lever).** A 2026 meta-analysis in adults with ADHD (Xu, Zhao & Hu, *Psychology of Sport and Exercise*; 14 trials reviewed, 8 pooled) found that a single bout of exercise produced a moderate improvement in inhibitory control (Hedges' g = 0.55) and a small improvement in core symptoms (g = 0.23). Chronic-training results were mixed.\[64\]\[65\] **Practical use:** 20–30 minutes of moderate cardio *before* the day's hardest cognitive block, especially in amber/red zones.
- **CBT and skills-based structure (strongest non-drug evidence).**
  - Young, Moghaddam & Tickle (2020, *J Atten Disord*): CBT beat waitlist (SMD 0.76) and active controls (SMD 0.43).\[66\]
  - Knouse et al. (2017), 32 studies: pre-post effects on self-reported symptoms of g = 1.00, smaller against active controls.\[67\]

  Your app *is* a CBT-style external structure tool. Implementation intentions, task breakdown, and time-boxing are the active ingredients.
- **Sleep and circadian timing (moderate evidence; the biggest perimenopause amplifier).** Van Andel, Kooij et al. ran an RCT of 51 adults with ADHD and delayed sleep phase:\[68\]
  - 0.5 mg melatonin advanced circadian timing by about 1.5 hours.\[69\]
  - It reduced ADHD symptoms by 14%.\[69\]
  - The benefit had disappeared 2 weeks after stopping.\[69\]
  - Bright light added to melatonin did not further reduce symptoms.\[69\]

  **Conclusion:** consistent sleep timing matters, and gains need ongoing maintenance. Night sweats and LOOP-cycle estrogen surges will disrupt sleep. Downgrade the next day's work when sleep is poor (rule below).
- **Morning light exposure:** mechanistically sensible for circadian anchoring. Direct evidence in adult ADHD is weak; the light arm above did not add benefit.\[70\] Low cost, low certainty.
- **Protein and blood sugar:** I found no trial evidence that protein-forward meals improve ADHD symptoms in adults. Steady meals plausibly reduce energy crashes and support mood. Treat this as reasonable self-care, not an evidence-based ADHD intervention.

**Medication context only:** de Jong et al. (2023) and Zaritsky et al. (2025) suggest stimulant effects may be blunted around menstruation or the premenstrual week.\[29\]\[71\] Clinics in the Netherlands are piloting premenstrual dose adjustment. For perimenopausal executive-function complaints, Epperson and Shanmugan's trials of lisdexamfetamine and atomoxetine in non-ADHD midlife women showed benefit.\[33\]\[72\] Hormone therapy is now a clinical consideration for women over 40 with ADHD and new or worsening cognitive and mood symptoms (de Jong et al., 2025, *European Psychiatry*).\[73\] This is for a conversation with your clinician, not self-management.

### 6. Testosterone, LH, and FSH: energy, motivation, verbal fluency, libido, confidence

**Testosterone:** daily-serum mass spectrometry in 25 women (Bui et al., 2013, *Steroids*) found testosterone "higher mid-cycle, but a peak was not discernible in each individual." The rise was "not clinically relevant since day-to-day variation is higher."\[74\] Roney & Simmons (2013, *Hormones and Behavior*) measured daily saliva in 43 women. Sexual desire peaked mid-cycle, driven by estradiol (positive) and progesterone (negative). There were "no significant effects of testosterone" once those were controlled.\[75\] **Conclusion:** the popular claim that "ovulatory testosterone makes you bolder and hornier" is mostly about estradiol. Testosterone's cyclic contribution is small.

**Verbal fluency:** Hampson (1990) found better articulatory and fine-motor speed in the late follicular, high-estradiol phase.\[76\] The perimenstrual estradiol RCT above showed estradiol prevented perimenstrual drops in verbal fluency. But the broader literature is inconsistent. Sundström Poromaa & Gingnell (2014) describe cycle differences as "small and difficult to replicate."\[37\] The 2025 PLOS ONE meta-analysis (Jang, Zhang & Elfenbein; 102 articles, N = 3,943) found "no systematic robust evidence for significant cycle shifts" in cognitive performance.\[7\]

**LH and FSH:** no good evidence that they act directly on cognition or motivation in humans. They are useful as *timing markers*, not as performance drivers.

**Social confidence:** there is no robust primary evidence that it rises with any hormone. Any late-follicular "social window" in the protocol rests on estradiol-mood links, not on testosterone.

**My position:** schedule presentations and negotiations late follicular when you can, because mood and emotional regulation tend to be better and the estradiol-withdrawal windows are avoided. Don't expect a dramatic cognitive edge. The bigger payoff is avoiding high-stakes social work during your own worst days.

## Part 2 — The 32-Day Protocol

**Assumptions:**
- 32-day cycle; LH surge about day 17; ovulation about day 18; luteal phase about 14 days. Real-world luteal phases are often 12–13 days, so the premenstrual drop may begin about 2 days earlier.
- Early perimenopause and LNG-IUD in place; no ADHD medication.
- LNG is a low, steady background on every day. It is listed once here rather than repeated in the progesterone column.
- Levels are *relative within your cycle*, not absolute lab values.

**Key:** ↑ rising, ↓ falling, → flat. **Zones:** 🟢 green = protect for hardest/highest-stakes work; 🟡 amber = execution with scaffolding; 🔴 red = buffer/low-demand.

| 30 | Premenstrual | ↓ steep | ↓ steep | FSH ↑ | Baseline | 🔴 Estradiol/progesterone withdrawal: highest mood risk, PMDD window, poor working memory | Rest/low-demand; routine tasks; protect sleep |
| 31 | Premenstrual | Low | Low | FSH ↑ | Baseline | 🔴 As above; brain fog | Low-demand; reschedule hard conversations |
| 32 | Premenstrual | Lowest | Lowest | FSH ↑ | Baseline | 🔴 As above | Low-demand; reflective planning only |

**Evidence grade of the table:**
- **Estradiol-withdrawal windows (days 19–20, 28–32, 1–3) as risk days:** preliminary-to-moderate. Converging small studies plus a causal RCT in a non-ADHD psychiatric sample.
- **Mid-follicular as the best window:** preliminary. One ADHD pilot plus mechanism.
- **Late-follicular "social/verbal" advantage:** weak. Mostly estradiol-mood and desire links; cognitive meta-analysis is null.
- **Hormone curves themselves:** well established for regular ovulatory cycles; less reliable in perimenopause.

### Where perimenopause can break this pattern

1. **Anovulatory cycle.** No LH surge and no temperature rise by about day 22. Then no luteal progesterone rise occurs, estradiol may stay moderate or wander, and bleeding (if any) can arrive unpredictably. **Action:** stay on a "follicular-style" schedule by default (green/amber mix) and let your daily ratings, not the calendar, decide. Treat any sudden drop in mood or focus as a possible withdrawal event and switch to red for 2–3 days.
2. **LOOP / estrogen-surge cycles.** Breast tenderness, insomnia, irritability, or heavy bleeding in the late luteal or menstrual days. **Action:** treat it as red for sleep protection, and shift days 1–3 even more conservative.
3. **Short luteal phase (under 11 days).** Your premenstrual dip arrives earlier. **Action:** if bleeding or symptoms show up before day 30, retro-date and count luteal length from the confirmed ovulation day. Store your personal luteal length in the app and use it to project the next red zone.
4. **Shortened follicular phase.** Early LH surge (e.g., day 13–14). **Action:** shift the entire grid backward so the LH-positive day = day 17.

### Re-anchoring when the IUD hides your period

- **Primary anchor = first positive LH test → label it "Day 17."** The app recomputes all days, assuming ovulation = Day 18.
- **Confirmation = temperature rise sustained for 3 days.** The first day of the rise is roughly the day after ovulation. If temperature never rises, flag the cycle "possibly anovulatory."
- **Backup anchor = symptom onset of premenstrual cluster** (mood drop, brain fog). Project the next "Day 1" as 2 days later, or as your personal luteal length after the LH surge.
- **If bleeding does occur and is clearly a period** (not spotting), it can reset Day 1. But give LH/temperature priority when they disagree.

## Setting this up in Day Time (app logic)

**1. Zone-based block templates (not 32 unique days).**
- 🟢 Green template: 2 × 90-minute deep-work blocks in the morning; meetings clustered on 2 afternoons; one "launch/pitch" slot on days 15–17.
- 🟡 Amber template: 3 × 45-minute execution blocks with checklists; meetings capped (e.g., 2 per day); end-of-day "close loops" block.
- 🔴 Red template: 1 × 30-minute priority block; admin and routine; no new commitments, negotiations, or major decisions; a protected early bedtime.

**2. Daily 30-second check-in.** Rate focus, mood, energy and sleep hours (1–5 scales), plus optional flags: hot flash or night sweat, breast tenderness, LH result, bleeding/spotting, exercise done. After 2–3 cycles, compute your average by cycle day relative to the LH surge. Retire this template wherever your data disagree.

**3. "If symptoms differ, then…" rules:**
- If sleep was under 6 hours or you had night sweats, then downgrade today by one zone (green → amber, amber → red).
- If you're in a green day but focus is ≤2/5 by 10 a.m., then swap the deep-work block for a 20–30 minute cardio session followed by a 45-minute attempt. If it's still ≤2, convert to an amber day.
- If you're in an amber or red day but feel 4–5/5, then pull one deferred deep-work task forward, but don't book new high-stakes commitments based on one good day.
- If mood is ≤2 for 2+ consecutive days at any point, then switch to red and prompt "possible estradiol-withdrawal or anovulatory event." Log it.
- If there's no LH positive by day 22, then mark the cycle "possibly anovulatory" and run green/amber by daily ratings.
- If premenstrual symptoms start before day 29 in two consecutive cycles, then shorten your personal luteal setting and move the red zone earlier.
- If severe premenstrual mood symptoms (hopelessness, rage, thoughts of self-harm) occur, then the app should surface a "talk to a clinician" prompt. Two cycles of prospective daily ratings is exactly the evidence clinicians need to assess PMDD, and treatment exists.

**4. Calibration period.** Run this template for **2–3 full cycles**, mainly as a measurement tool. Individual variation is large. Hormone-sensitivity research consistently finds that only a subset of women show strong cycle-linked symptoms, and that within-person tracking is the only valid way to see it.\[77\]\[78\]

## Caveats

- **Almost none of the cycle research matches you.** The studies are mostly in women aged 18–40, many without ADHD, and none in LNG-IUD users or perimenopausal women. Every cell in the protocol table that predicts ADHD effects is an extrapolation.
- **Sample sizes are small:** 32 (Roberts), 30 (Zaritsky), 9 (de Jong), 24 (Jacobs & D'Esposito).\[14\] PMDD prevalence figures come from screening and self-report, not prospective diagnosis.
- **Perimenopause and ADHD studies conflict.** One cohort finds more severe symptoms; another finds no difference. Surveys are self-selected.
- **Cognitive performance effects of the cycle are not robust at the population level.** The protocol's value lies mostly in managing load around mood and emotional regulation, and in pacing deep work. It is not about hitting "peak IQ days."
- **Non-medication evidence varies:** exercise and CBT/structure are supported. Light exposure and dietary protein/blood-sugar strategies are reasonable but not proven for adult ADHD.
- **This is not medical advice.** Persistent mood symptoms, very heavy or prolonged bleeding, or new cognitive decline warrant clinical assessment. So do questions about hormone therapy or ADHD medication.
